Wait, You Play Gloomhaven Competitively?

(10 min. read)

There’s someone in my life who can (something funny about Star here) He also really likes Gloomhaven. One time I was an I pugilistic mood and decided to tell him everything I didn’t like about Gloomhaven’s design, you know because I’m such a good friend. Three beers later I’m in full rant mode going off on this game and throw out “Oh and the semi-cooperative rules are super tacked on. They don’t fit the game at all. If it’s a game partially about narrative those mechanics don’t work at all.” As I kept yelling about Gloomhaven my friend raised an eyebrow confused about that point. You see, that rule was one of the parts of Gloomhaven he finds very interesting, but I hated it.

For context, Gloomhaven is a skirmish game where you play little D&D-like heroes in various scenarios strung together in a campaign. For example you might have a fight against some slimes, and after you read a paragraph saying the slimes were made by an evil wizard. So the next scenario is a fight against that evil wizard. Each combat is a self-contained little puzzle, but a narrative connects them all together. You succeed/fail the combat as a group, but any gold/xp you gain is yours alone. It’s possible for the group to win, but only one player has gotten all the gold/XP. So while every player wants to win, everyone theoretically wants as much gold/XP for themself. Gold/XP is used to make your character more powerful. Many players in Gloomhaven love optimizing their character so they will even put the scenario is slight jeopardy if it means they might level up faster. They don’t care if their friends level up or not, they want to level up themselves.

That's how the game is supposed to work, and this is how my friend played the game. My group did not play this way at all: that’s the source of my friends earlier confusion. My group would never act selfishly when it comes to gold/XP. We don’t fight each other for it, we actually agree before the scenario begins who could best use it. Let’s say one character needs more mobility, we will make sure they get gold to buy blink-shoes. Or maybe one character is about to unlock a powerful upgrade, so we make sure they get the XP. We don’t care who gets the rewards, we just want our group as a whole to be as powerful as possible. One time I went multiple sessions without advancing because it made sense for me to “fall on a sword” to protect the person who was quickly getting more powerful.

This is the specific rule that felt weird to us: the game forbids players trading gold with each other. It didn’t make sense that we were risking our lives for each other day after day, but can’t just give a helmet to our ally. We understood why the game needed the rule for balance, but narrative-wise the rule stook out like a sore thumb.

3 Incentives: New Content, Overcoming Difficult Challenges, and Fear of Losing

We have a situation where two groups read the exact same set of rules, but play the game totally differently. One plays entirely cooperatively, helping each other and sacrificing for the good of the group, while the other is cut-throat advance yourself at all costs. And this is not one group misreading the mechanics, we both played the game totally correctly as written. It’s just that players were motivated by different things; the two groups didn’t misread the words of the text, they misread the meaning between the words. The rules in this game are almost a “Ludic Rorschach Test” where multiple people see the same mechanics and draw different conclusions about how the game should be played.

This is a big deal, if multiple players mis-read the incentives of a game that game fully becomes degenerate. As an extreme example we could consider a basketball team that’s incentivized not to have a higher score than the opponent, but as high a score as possible, regardless of what the other team scored. They might be able to play a functional game of basketball, but the high scoring team doesn't care about defense, only going full tilt on offense. Without agreed-upon incentives games cease to function. I would go as far to say any definition of a game would need to include something referring to “agreed upon incentives”. The second half of this essay will be analyzing how the incentives got muddled in Gloomhaven’s case.

The goal of a game is to win, but that’s not the reason people play games. In fact, I think especially among hobbyist board gamers, winning is not the reason we play games. We are always trying to win, but it’s not as if winning is required to have fun. (If winning were required to have fun most games would only have a 25% fun percentage). There are other reasons folks play games, one I want to focus on is a sense of progress. In Gloomhaven, it’s very fun to level up and unlock new abilities. It’s fun to choose which new ability best combos with your existing build and optimize this, and it’s just fun to see new content. This doesn’t even mention the most fun form of advancement in Gloomhaven, when your character reaches max level you get to unlock an entirely new character, and you are the first person who gets to play with them. Your reward for fully leveling up, is a special class no one else has seen and no one else gets to use. That, in itself, is very fun. I believe the folks who are driven to advance their character as much as possible view this as the most pleasurable reward in the game.

This is not how I think, when I play Gloomhaven I never consider how fun it will be to unlock more abilities and a new class. The sense of accomplishment I get from winning a difficult scenario is the most pleasurable reward. I would take defeating a difficult scenario over a new character any day of the week.

This is where I think the mismatched incentives come from, different players perceive these rewards differently, and so adjust their play accordingly. One group believes new characters to be a most pleasureable, the other believes beating a tough encounter to be the most important. There is one more giant incentive that I haven’t mentioned yet.

By far the absolute worst design in Gloomhaven, in my opinion, is what happens when you lose a scenario. There are no fail-forward mechanics. If the group fails a scenario, rules-as-written they have to try again. This makes losing the worst part of any night of Gloomhaven.

Variety is the spice of life in these scenario based games, redoing the same one when there's literally 40 more still in the box you haven't seen yet feels awful, especially when those combats can take multiple hours. My group of team players plays on a much higher difficulty than my friends group of cutthroat powergamers. So, unlike them, we are often in danger of losing if we don’t play well. And again, losing is so painful. I believe that if a gold-hungry player showed up to our table we would see their actions as unsportsmanlike. Losing is so boring and lame in Gloomhaven that being greedy at our table would be rude. We would hate to lose a scenario because someone was being a jerk and taking the gold for themselves.

So that’s the third massive incentive that drives my group. Even if some players might like to unlock new abilities and characters, prioritizing that is soured by how bitter losing a scenario is. If in a players mind unlocking new characters and winning a hard challenge are about equally desirable, the bad feelings from potentially losing would fully tip the incentives into winning a hard challenge. The groups that prioritize character advancement are probably playing on a low enough difficulty there isn’t a real danger of losing the scenario.

Aside about "Pretending you Won"

Losing in Gloomhaven feels bad, it feels bad enough that if they lose a scenario many groups will just pretend they won and move on, especially if this is not the first time they've lost to this scenario. But it doesn't take a genius to see why this is unsatisfying. By moving on as if they had won, the players are harming the narrative being told. The game had an initial promise "complete my puzzles, unlock more narrative", ignoring that feels like betraying a fundamental premise of the game. The narrative feels less impactful when you didn't "earn it". At the end of the day, the narrative is just tacked on to the side of the game, but acknowledging that feels like it crosses a line. Yeah we all know the story doesn't matter, but you can't come out and "say it".

I think the designers should have just written a story more fit to the mechanics. The videogame Pyre that did this well despite not having a large branching narrative by having wins/losses only affect the character arcs, not larger story. Or do what Warhammer does and make the universe so big any potential scenario is always canon. A more straightforward solution would be creating a mechanic to track wins/losses. Maybe every loss adds a "Doom" to the town and if the players hit 5/10/15 doom there's an optional scenario to undo the damage. You could even give a unique ending for players who fail a doom scenario. I believe some good writers could make the Doom endings at least as compelling as the main ending.

Determining the Weights of a Games Incentives

Players come to games trying to win, but seeking something else. Players come to a game because it offers them something besides victory. We’ve talked about 3 incentives in Gloomhaven, and how they are weighted differently by different players. Here’s a hypothetical: if Gloomhaven did have the fail forward system I described with the Doom counters, how would the incentives balance out for my group? Would we continue to be win-at-all-cost gamers, or would a decreased fear of losing drive us to be a little more greedy and XP-hungry? This is an impossible question to answer but I have some thoughts about ways a designer could put their thumb on the scales to encourage particular incentives.
