Wait, You Play Gloomhaven Competitively?

(10 min. read)

A few months ago I was at home, restless and just itching for an argument. Maybe the urge to fight came from too many boring days at work or some deep desire to go to war. Either way, my prayer was answered when I saw two friends of mine playing Gloomhaven in a Discord call. Gloomhaven—perfect! Not only do I have issues with it, but it’s also an incredibly popular title, so by ripping it apart I can maintain my contrarian status! So, like a hero, I joined the Discord call and hijacked the conversation to air my grievances.[cite: 1]

One of these friends has Gloomhaven as his absolute number one favorite of all time, so he took a lot of exception to my criticisms. Two hours later neither of us had convinced the other, go figure, but I did learn something unexpected: this friend, who plays irl with a different group, approaches the title entirely differently than my group did. I’d go as far as to say the culture of play between our groups contrasted so sharply that a player from one would struggle to fit in with the other. There would be a massive culture clash.[cite: 1]

For context, Gloomhaven isn’t a very complicated title conceptually. This is not the sort of release you’d expect to have distinct subcultures surrounding it. It’s a skirmish tactical adventure about Dungeons & Dragons-like heroes fighting monsters. You do your skirmishes, read a paragraph of text, then move onto the next encounter; rinse and repeat. One scenario might pit you against slimes in a cave. Then, after winning, you read that “the cave leads down into an ancient city, where the wizard who made these slimes lives,” and suddenly you’ve teleported to the top of a wizard tower. You get the idea.[cite: 1]

The core loop has players working together to clear a string of self-contained encounters, with one tiny hiccup: any XP and treasure you grab is yours to keep. Rewards are not split evenly among the party. So, while everyone wants to win the overall scenario, every player wants to maximize personal gains during combat. You’re on the same team, but you aren't working together 100% of the time.[cite: 1]

The reason players work against group interests for XP is simple: leveling up is fun. It’s satisfying to pick out a new upgrade to optimize your build and grow more powerful. The fantasy of progression powers roughly 80% of AAA video games nowadays; it's a potent incentive. But there’s an even bigger hook in Gloomhaven that tempts you to betray your companions: when your character reaches max level, you unlock an entirely new class exclusively for you that no one else can use. My friend noted that people in his group would regularly jeopardize a scenario if it meant securing extra XP to unlock their next toy. The siren song of fresh content lures players into acting selfishly.[cite: 1]

Apparently that’s how the system is intended to work, but it highlights a massive divide because my group never plays that way. We never fight each other for gold or XP; instead, we coordinate to distribute resources to whoever can use them best. If someone needs mobility, we orchestrate the battle to ensure they secure the Charged Boots. If someone is closing in on a powerful ability, we all pitch in to help them hit their milestones. It’s not uncommon for a player to end a session with zero gold or XP because they spent the entire night supporting others. We play entirely cooperatively, stripping away the competitive elements my friend’s circle embraces.[cite: 1]

When we played fully cooperatively, one particular regulation always felt out of place: players are strictly forbidden from trading items after a battle ends. We recognized the rule existed for balance, but it clashed with the narrative tone. Here I am jumping in front of cannon fire and getting stabbed so my companion can open a treasure chest, but if I happen to scavenge extra leather, I can't just hand it over so they can craft shoes—don’t be ridiculous. Perhaps we were supposed to treat this restriction as an intentional nudge toward competitiveness, but we wrote it off as subpar design instead.[cite: 1]

Different Incentives

Two groups read the exact same rulebook and played completely differently. One group is motivated by winning at all costs, while the other prioritizes scaling power and unlocking content. Neither group misread the rules; everything was executed by the letter of the law. They simply reached different conclusions regarding the spirit of the law. That shift in interpretation yields two radically different table experiences: selfless teamwork versus cutthroat self-preservation. The rules function almost like a Magic Eye picture—every group perceives a completely different set of incentives when they look closer.[cite: 1]

This is a big deal. If different people can read identical rules and interpret entirely separate incentives, it has huge ramifications. Player alignment on incentives is arguably the single most important element in any hobby. Our Gloomhaven example is ultimately harmless, but it mirrors higher-stakes design failures. Consider a basketball match where teams disagree on fundamentals: one side plays normally, while the other just wants to rack up points without playing defense. They could technically share a court, but it wouldn't be satisfying. The all-offense team would likely grow furious at opponents burning clock through passing rather than shooting immediately. Both sides would leave frustrated.[cite: 1]

I imagine some readers are confused. “The goal is to win; both groups agree on that, so what’s the problem?” Of course winning is the objective, but saying that’s *why* people play is reductive. Why pull Gloomhaven off the shelf instead of Catan? There is a deeper motivation at play. You might be playing for the satisfaction of progression, to unlock box content, or to engineer crazy ability combos. I bet the euro-game crowd loves it purely for those mechanical synergies.[cite: 1]

Those are all valid reasons to enjoy an experience beyond winning: leveling up, discovering new items, and executing combos are genuinely fun. My friend’s circle clearly valued those elements most. But my group calculated fun differently: we decided that conquering difficult scenarios was paramount, progression be damned. We wanted to face the hardest challenges possible and scrape by. To achieve this, we played on higher difficulties than my friends did, regularly teetering on the edge of defeat. The magic for us was that heartbeat of realization—“oh no, we’re going to lose”—followed by a miraculous comeback. I’d take winning a brutal fight over unlocking a new class any day.[cite: 1]

Fear of Losing

Because we played on punishing difficulties, my group developed a genuine fear of losing that my friend’s group lacked. And let me tell you: by far the worst thing that can happen in Gloomhaven is failure. It’s not that the game imposes heavy economic penalties; instead, it does something much worse—it does nothing. It simply resets the board and asks you to replay a grueling two-hour session from scratch.[cite: 1]

Variety is the spice of life in scenario-based campaigns. Redoing a map when there are literally forty untouched challenges waiting in the box feels awful. Winding up a night on a loss is a miserable experience because it feels like a waste of time, often boiling down to minor tactical adjustments—like target prioritization over boss focus. Once you know the optimal script, re-running the map isn't hard; it's just tedious.[cite: 1]

My group wanted the threat of failure to keep stakes high, but we despised actually failing—especially when caused by a greedy teammate ditching objectives for treasure. If someone from my friend’s group tried that at our table, we wouldn't want to play with them again. Even if players enjoy unlocking new toys, that satisfaction gets poisoned by how punishing a defeat feels. When the misery of losing outweighs the thrill of new character unlocks, the psychological incentives tilt heavily back toward pure survival.[cite: 1]

Aside about "Pretending you Won"

Losing feels bad enough that many gaming groups simply skip failed scenarios instead of retrying. That’s a valid approach, I suppose, but it doesn't take a genius to see why it feels hollow. While everyone prefers organic victories, skipping defeats damages the overarching narrative. Gloomhaven is ultimately a sequence of skirmishes held together by a loose story thread, and skipping lost fights shines a glaring spotlight on those seams. Sure, we know it's a disjointed string of encounters, but you shouldn't spell it out so bluntly. Better solutions exist that require far less mental gymnastics.[cite: 1]

For one, the writing could have accommodated mechanical failures from the start. The video game Pyre handled this masterfully: its plot doesn't branch based on binary success, but individual character arcs shift dynamically based on wins and losses. The macro-story remains intact while its texture adapts to player performance through localized dialogue adjustments. Alternatively, you could take a page from Warhammer and make the universe so vast that any outcome becomes canon, framing each session as a vignette with loose narrative ties.[cite: 1]

A more straightforward fix without rewriting the script would be a system tracking wins and losses. Imagine if every defeat added "Doom" to the town, triggering an optional crisis scenario once thresholds hit 5, 10, or 15. You could craft unique conclusions for players who fail those crisis runs, making failure an interesting narrative branch rather than a dead end. Ultimately, “pretending you won” is a lazy patch for a fixable problem.[cite: 1]

Tinkering with Incentives

People approach hobbies hoping to win, but they are usually chasing deeper psychological rewards. What you want out of an experience shapes how you play. I’ve detailed how my group forged our style, but consider a hypothetical: if Gloomhaven featured that Doom system, would my table change behavior? Personally, I think some of us would shift from win-at-all-costs tacticians to greedy looters, forcing us to reconcile clashing objectives.[cite: 1]

Ultimately, things get muddled in Gloomhaven because it throws multiple conflicting progression loops at you simultaneously (advancement, challenge-seeking, synergy optimization). Tighter designs avoid this trap. Take a traditional cooperative title like Pandemic; people don't play it to build combos or unlock hidden characters. Even in Pandemic Legacy, when new content arrives, it belongs to the whole table rather than individual progression tracks. Pandemic lacks personal advancement loops, meaning player incentives remain unified: beat the challenge through teamwork, and nothing else.[cite: 1]

I could design a set of weird, conflicting win conditions where player choices mirror their underlying values. The system would act as a literal Rorschach test, holding up a mirror to the table.
