Wait, You Play Gloomhaven Competitively?

(15 min. read)

A few months ago I was at home, restless and just itching for an argument. Maybe the urge to fight came from too many boring days at work or some deep desire to go to war. Either way my prayer was answered when I saw two friends of mine playing Gloomhaven in a Discord call. Gloomhaven–perfect! Not only do I have issues with that game, it’s also an incredibly popular game so by ripping it apart I can maintain my contrarian status! So, like a hero, I joined the Discord call to hijack the conversation.

For the record, Gloomhaven's combat system is great, I have no notes there. I just do not like other parts of the design. One of these friends in the discord call however, has Gloomhaven as his absolute #1 favorite game of all time, so him disagreeing with me I guess shouldn't have been a surprise. What was a surprise though was learning he played the game totally different from me. I’d go as far to say each of our groups play so differently it'd be near impossible for players to go from one group to the other.

For context, Gloomhaven isn’t a very complicated game conceptually. "How do you play?" shouldn't be a hard question to answer. It's a very straight forward skirmish game where you play as about Dungeons & Dragons-like heroes. You do your little fights, read a paragraph of text, then move onto the next fight; rinse and repeat. One fight is against some slimes in a cave, you read that following the cave downward leads to the wizard who made the slimes, the next fight is against the wizard. You get it.

The game is just players working together to kill things. The game does add one tiny hiccup to this formula: any XP you gain and any treasure you grab is yours to keep; rewards are not split evenly among the party. This means everyone wants to win the conflict, but every player wants to maximize their personal rewards as much as possible. You’re on the same team, but you aren't working together 100% of the time.

The reason players will work against the interest group interests for XP is simple: leveling up is fun. Who doesn't love picking out a new upgrade and optimizing a build? Who doesn't love getting more powerful? Dopamine from getting strongre powers like 80% of AAA video games nowadays for a reason; people love it. It really gets the people going when they learn they now deal +2 more damage while flanking. But there’s actually an even bigger hook in Gloomahven that tempts you to betray your friends: when your character reaches max level you unlock an new character no one else can use. My friend noted players in his irl group often put the fight in mild jeopardy of losing just so they can get extra XP because they wanted to unlock their new toy. The siren song of new content lures players into act selfishly.

Apparently that’s how the game is supposed to work, and not at all how my group plays. This is where the divide between the two groups comes from. My group would never fight each other for gold/XP; instead we always work together to distribute gold/XP to the people that can use it best. If one person needs mobility we coordinate the fight to make sure they get the Charged Boots. If someone is about to unlock a powerful ability we all help to make sure they get their XP. It’s not uncommon for one player to end up with no gold/XP at all because they were trying to help another character as much as possible. We play the game entirely cooperatively, ignoring the competitive elements my friend's group loves.

When we were playing fullly cooperatively, one particular rule often felt really lame and out of place: players can't trade items with each other. We recognize this rule exists for balnce reasons, but it felt really weird narratively that we'd jump in front of canons and get stabbed protecting each other, but would never give our ally an extra piece of leather to mak some boots. I'm willing to admit maybe this ludo-narrative dissonance probably should've been a canary's call that we were playing the game different than designer intent, but it never really crossed our minds that this game was supposed to be competative in any way.

Different Incentives

Two groups read the exact same rulebook, and played completely differently: why? One group is motivated by winning the scenario at all costs, while the other group wants to get more powerful and unlock new content, and neither group actually misread any rules. Technically, in the eye of the law, no rules were ever broken. Both groups followed the letter of the law, but had different interpretations on the spirit of the law. The rules in this game are almost like a Magic Eye Picture; every group sees a different set of incentives when they look at it.

This is a big deal. If players can read identical rules, but produce different incentives from it, that can make the game itself degenerate. Consider two basketball teams disagreeing on incentives: one team is playing normally, while the other wants to rack up points without playing defense at all. These two teams could technically play a game of basketball together, but it wouldn't be a satisfying game. The all-offense team would likely get upset with the normal team for burning clock through passing instead of just shooting immediately. You could imagine them saying "they aren't playing the game correctly, you're ruining it for us by wasting all this time." Players agreeing on incentives is probably the single most important thing in any game.

I imagine some folk reading this are very confused. “The goal of Gloomhaven is to win; both of the groups agree on that, so what’s the problem?” Well, no. Of course the goal of the game is to win, but it’s reductive to say that’s why people play. Why do you pull Gloomhaven off the shelf instead of Catan? There’s some motivation here besides winning. You might be playing because you like getting stronger, or you want to unlock all the hidden content in the box. Or maybe you're just a euro-gamer and want to make a bunch of combos.

Those are all valid reasons to enjoy a game beyond winning. My friend's group just made their calculus of what they enjoyed differently. They wanted to build combos and unlock new characters, meanwhile my group's primary motivation is to win a difficult scenario. The best moment at our game nights is that hopeless moment of “oh no we are going to lose”, then some clever play reverses the situation and we win anyway. I personally would take winning a difficult fight over a new character any day of the week. However, there was one other massive incentive that was tipping the scales for my group.

Fear of Losing

My group played on a much higher difficulty than my friend's group did. So when my friend's group put the scenario in mild jeopardy to get some treasure, the odds of them actually losing because of it are still pretty low. Meanwhile my group had losing as an option on the table almost constantly. And here's the thing with difficulty in Gloomhaven: by far the absolute worst thing that can happen to you in Gloomhaven is to lose a fight. It’s not like you are heavily punished for losing with heavy penalties. It’s actually much worse: the game doesn’t do anything if you lose a fight. It just resets everything and asks you to try again: after you just got done playing out a 2 hour fight.

Variety is the spice of life in these scenario-based games. Redoing the same fight you just did when there's literally 40 untouched challenges waiting in the box feels awful. Ending a night on a loss, makes coming back the next week and redoing it feel like such a waste of time, usually because it only requires a slight change in tactos. Maybe instead of focusing the minions you now focus the boss. Once you realize the optimal strategy, actually executing can feel tedius and annoying.

My group wants the threat of failure, but we super hate it when it happens. And we would certainly dislike someone who caused us to lose a fight because they were going for treasure instead of killing enemies. This where the culture clash I mentioned earlier would come from if people tried to move groups. If someone from my friends group showed up and started being greedy, we'd conider them a jerk and not want to play with them anymore. Players in my group might even secretly love unlocking new characters, but would never prioritize it because of how bitter losing tastes in this game.

Aside about "Pretending you Won"

Losing feels so bad many groups just skip a scenario once they have failed it instead of retrying. That’s a valid way of playing I guess, but it doesn't take a genius to see why it’s unsatisfying. At the end of the day, sure, Gloomhaven is just a string of fights. Who cares if you skip one you don't want to retry. But actually trying to skip a fight when the narrative calls for it is acknowledging the story's tacked-on nature in a way that's a little gauche: yeah sure we all know that, but you shouldn’t just come out and say it. More importantly, the game doesn't need to work this way. There are ways to design aroun dthis problem.

For one, this game could've just told its story in a different way. They could've done what Warhammer does and make the universe so big that everything is canon. There's probably a place somewhere that the Tyranids fought the Space Marines on an ice world, sure. This frames each individual combat as a vignette of a much larger story. The actual outcome doesn't matter, what matters is that this totally could've happened. People love warhammer lore so clearly this sort of method can work. Another option is to tailor the story to accomodate wins/losses organically. The videogame Pyre did this well: the combats are not battles-to-the-death, but something closer to sport. Winning and losing doesn't mean the characters die, it means lost credibility or money or whatever resource is being fought over. Maybe after losing a fight the players don't have enough money to pay for Tiny Tim's surgury so he will spend the rest of his days limping. The story will still continue on with a crippled Tiny Tim, but it's actual impact on the board story is minimal. Losing the fight had more of a flavorful impact that a strict one. This way the player's actions clearly have consequences, but it doesn't require forking the story multiple times to create.

A more straightforward fix without rewritting the story would be a system that tracks wins/losses. Imagine if every loss added a "Doom" to the town, triggering a special scenario once players hit 5/10/15 doom. If players win the doom scenario, the damage is undone. But if the players fail a doom scenario they unlock a unique ending, and if they succeed the doom scenario it's as if nothing happened. You could even add a rule where if players fail a scenario they actually have a choice between adding a doom vs retrying the scenario. Players who just want to move on can have narrative consequences for their failure, and players who don't mind retrying can go for their "perfect" run. Ultimately, my point is “pretend you won” is a bad solution to a fixable problem.

Tinkering with Incentives

People approach games trying to win, but are usually chasing some other desire (build combos, face a hard challenge, unlock content). What you want out of an expereince shapes the way you play. I've detailed how my group came to our "afraid of losing" playstyle, but consider a hypothetical: if Gloomhaven had the Doom system I just described, would my table change our behavior? Personally, I think some of us would shift from "win-at-all-costs" into more greedy progression-focused gamers. I think with only a slight shift in the rules, our incentives would change.

Ultimately, things get incentives get muddled in Gloomhaven because it throws mutliple reasons to play in front of its players. More straightforward games don't have this problem. No one plays Pandemic to build combos or unlock new content. Even in Pandemic Legacy, new content is not locked to one player like it is in Gloomhaven so there is no in-fighting. Pandemic Legacy doesn't have that much personal advancemnt, combos, etc. The only reason to play panemic is to work together to beat the challenge, there's nothing else there, so incentives are not muddled.

All of this got my brain turning. If Gloomhaven did this on accident, could I do this on purpose? Could I create a game that by design had unclear incentives, to behave as a weird ludic-Rorschach test? I could make a set of weird win conditions, and depending on what players choose it would say something about what they value as players; The way you react to and play the game is a mirror into your own brain. And wouldn’t it be really funny if this extremely experimental game was a rules variant for Wingspan?
