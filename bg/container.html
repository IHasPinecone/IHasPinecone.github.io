REMEMBER WHAT THEY TOOK FROM YOU

(10 min. read)

When you buy something in Monopoly, where does the money go? Who gets it? Some unknown mysterious real estate company? Are you satisfied if the game just says “The Bank”? When you wrap around the board in Monopoly to collect $200, where does the money come from? Is it from this same vague “Bank”.

The bank's purpose is obvious, it is an abstraction for some market, but that's not all it does. The bank also works to squeeze freedom and player agency and control the game. With a bank moderating things, things are no-nonsense; players can’t sell hotels to each other or empty the chance card money. With a bank, these things are not in the players hands, but the designers. The designer can freely tweak and balance the game to fit their intended experience; not yours. Doesn’t that piss you off? Doesn’t that get you mad? They don't want you to see this. See what they are taking from you. See what games you could be playing, without those that are smothering your freedom. Designers who attach so much of their game to the bank are, frankly, cowards.

There are some designers that live a little more dangerously. They know that when you have a dog, you don't chain them up so it can only trot in the small area, you should unleash that dog! Let it run as hard and as fast as it wants, only then you'll see its full potential. The designers who think this way live for something more than monopoly’s set hotel prices and income. They dream of a wider world, one where no one knows how much something will cost, one where the changing whim of a single player could send the whole table into a death spiral or a new explosion of economic activity. In these games the economy is not something set by the designers, but a strange gumbo made by every player at the table. I am here to present one of these wild and dangerous games. You might ask ‘what is this game about?’ I’ll tell you; it’s about shipping cargo. Yeah I’m excited too.

Meet Container.

In this game players will be buying and selling crates from each other, but this ain’t no pussyfoot Monopoly with a ‘Go’ space. Nah, if you want to make money in this game, you need to find another player who will sell it to you at a good price, and another one that will buy it back from you at a higher price. The designers didn’t decide “these crates are $3” YOU did that. If you refuse to buy at $3 the other player might drop the price, or not. If you always buy at $3 they might raise the prices, or not. This game doesn’t shove players into specific behaviors, the game has built a world and asks players to find their place in it.

Ok but what’s all this buying and selling for? Glad you asked, players are trying to sell crates to the center island for points, and getting the crates there is not easy. First, crates need made in factories, then they need transported on trucks to warehouses, then they are picked up by ships, and then after a lengthy shipping time they arrive on the island where they are unloaded. So that’s about 4 hoops a crate needs to jump through for it to be scored. And here’s the kicker, at every step I just listed, players must buy and sell between to each other. You can't move the crate along by yourself. Every step requires a transaction. It must move from your spot, to another player's spot. No single player is allowed to bring a crate “from farm to table” by themselves.

Like I said this game doesn’t set prices for things, players do, you can imagine how player personality could radically change the market. My friend Jack is really stingy; he hates over spending. If prices are really high he will do everything he can to not buy anything. All the money sitting in his hand is effectively removed from the game. With less money available, everything will have to come down in price, not just the things he is holding out for. And the opposite is also true, my friend Ihor plays at a much higher velocity. He is always making high risk plays trying to surge as far ahead of everyone else as possible. He never hoards money, so the economy expands. With more money flowing around prices will keep climbing and climbing.

This all works because Container's economy is mostly “closed”; money doesn’t enter or leave, it only changes hands. Remember that in monopoly you’re always paying money to and taking income from the bank meanwhile in Container the money exclusively flows between players. Closed economies are frequently some of the most interesting game economies to examine, and a lot of that is how much they are player driven. Basically everything I've described so far just comes from the game having a closed economy. But that’s not the only reason I like Container, I think beyond that, the designers have made some great choices to encourage a more economically chaotic atmosphere. The next big piece of the pie is turn order.

Sidereal Confluence vs Container

Maybe the most beloved trading game of all time is Sidereal Confluence, and it’s obvious why people like it. Sidereal Confluence delivers on the promise of a truly open market. Every round there is a trading phase where every player literally stands up and starts running around the room making as many deals as possible. Everything happens in real time and with the right group it can be a very fun time, but I don’t like it.

See Sidereal Confluence is in fact a totally open market, and that removes all the hard edges. I’ve talked before about my love of miserable games. I love games that make your heart sink in despair, or give you a stabbing sensation when something doesn’t go to plan. In Container, with good play, your opponents can put you over a barrel. And that’s only really possible with a more strict turn structure.

Look at this market in Container. Only one player has crates, and the person after you really really wants crates because they want to fill up their ship. So you can buy the cheap crates, then list your own crates for a really absurd price. What are they gonna do? Are they going to just pass their turn out of principle? You might identify this player doesn’t have any good actions available to them besides picking up crates. If the only options are your expensive crates, they might just eat those giant prices because it’s better than passing their turn. You only get so many turns in this game, you don’t want to waste them. This cutthroat situation would be a lot rarer in Sidereal Confluence, because someone would jump in and try to outbid you for those crates you emptied from the market. You could never buy the low ones and sell your own for high. Everything would equalize to some average value.

That’s what a truly free and open market does: it finds the accurate price, and who wants a perfectly accurate price in a trading game? That’s not fun. What’s fun are the miserable points, being put over a barrel and trying to get out of it, or exploiting another vulnerable player for all they are worth. In Sidereal Confluence you just don’t get that much value per trade. The savviest trader in the world might be getting 5% or 10% extra value for every trade they do, but in Container a good player could double or triple the value they get from every trade. In the more freeform Sidereal Confluence the game isn’t about making the best trades, it’s making the most. It’s not quality, it’s just quantity. Sidereal Confluence is, ultimately, a game about who can be the loudest and talk the most. If you talk the most then you will make 100 trades, and beat the person who only made 80. There’s no hard edges to push your opponents against to squeeze extra value out of them. There’s no turn structure, ridiculous booms, or glorious busts. In Sidereal Confluence, the market is so open, that it feels lifeless.

Double or Nothing?

The last thing that makes this game really shine for me is that holding a lead in this game can be surprisingly hard. Let’s say you are winning early. What do you do? What is a ‘safe’ play that will let you just maintain your lead forever? I’ll give you a short answer: there isn’t one. A good play can look a lot like a bad one, and what looks like a safe play could fully ruin you. This is a recurring fact in every game that I love; you are always on the brink of disaster. If you feel comfortable in your lead, you’re about to be usurped. There’s many ways other games give you this sense of unease. Container does it with one simple question: Double or Nothing?

If you are winning early, other players will pass you if you do nothing. You always need to keep reinvesting, keep shooting for the gold, keep pushing hard. And if the wrong bet plays out poorly, it could send you all the way back down to the bottom.

A Small Criticism

I am going to briefly mention my biggest complaint with the game that’s also its greatest strength: the game is very simple. Even with its slow trickle of expansions that game is still incredibly rules light. You can teach it in 5 minutes and play for hours. But, with a game so simple, games will start feeling samey after a while. Remember how I said earlier the game feels very different with different tables of players? That was true, with different people the game plays very differently. But with the same players? Well it’s the same personality. over and over. There’s not going to be wide variance because the group will probably settle into some similar level over and over again. My group played this game a lot, but with the same people over and over it felt very stale. I will also mention at the end here Tokyo Tsukigi Market by Jordan DraperTokyo Tsukigi Market by Jordan Draper. That game is much more complicated than Container and has lots of variance game-to-game. It feels like a kickstarter spiritual successor to Container in a good way. If you did click that link though, you saw that it's sadly out of print. Plus, all the Tabletop Simulator mods are broken. For what it's worth there are lots of print and play assets out there if you're very dedicated. I think the game is 100% worth it.

I really like Container, and despite it being out of print for a long time, it is currently in print. It makes me so happy that this game is on store shelves again. It’s one of the best games I’ve ever played and certainly my favorite trading game, alongside Tokyo Tsukigi Market. I highly recommend.
