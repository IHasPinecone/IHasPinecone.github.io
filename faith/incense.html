Incense

(10 min. read)

Sometimes when I walk into a church it’s like being hit in the face. The previous distractions going through my mind about life or work or whatever is just knocked out of me. With that clarity I can stroll forward into the church focusing on the only thing that really matters at the end of the day: God. My body actually feels a physical reaction after I've stepped across that boundary, like some deep part of me recognizes that this moment is different, more meaningful, than the moments from the rest of the day. It doesn't happen every time, but praise God for when it does.
I love that quiet walk to my pew, my mind blank and just fully in the moment. Part of what puts me in this state is the church building itself; the construction and artwork are supposed to instill this feeling of importance. This place is different; it is separated from the World.

Praise God for the feeling of a still church. I'm not kidding when I say it feels different than any other building. The church is open, but not dry and cracky and like a gym. The openness isn't acoustically smothered like a theater either, you can actually hear your footsteps. But the footsteps aren't due to some tragic emptiness, but the history; I can feel all the faithful who worshipped here before me, praying here day after day. My grandparents felt this same way when they were my age, coming to pray. And this place isn't dusty or dirty like a warehouse, it's ageless. My steps are just one part of this unbroken chain, echoing through time.

And the incense. Oh the incense. This same incense is how my great-great-great-grandparents found God. The floral and spiced smell just naturally brings one into a prayerful state. Monks in the middle ages worshipping the same God, smelled the same fragrance. Businessmen in the roaring 20s would have slowed down when that aroma wafted into their nostrils. Third century mystics would've found that powerful fiery God in these same scents. The specific blends may have changed, but the feeling never did. Hundreds and hundreds of years Christians have been brought to God by the same smell of incense I feel when I step into a church.

Picture the thurible. You know what a thurible is: it's the big thing on a long silver chain that incense burns inside. I think they look pretty cool. Not gonna lie, there's a reason fantasy artists love drawing these things being wielded in their cool fantasy religions. In Catholicism the thurible is often used at Adoration. The Lord has just been exposed in the Eucharist, the whole congregation sits still as the priest moves and kneels before God. There's a moment of silence as he takes the incense and fills the thurible. Then, still kneeled in reverence before his Creator, the priest takes the thurible and flings it forward toward God. With each throw the thurible swings and billows out clouds, tracing arcs through the air. The clouds fly and thrust like an impressionist painter throwing paint, splashing wildly and erratically. As the thurible is cast, it's as it shouts “MORE TO YOU GOD! MORE TO YOU!” The thurible gives itself fully in its worship. With every swing it shouts and exhales “MORE GLORY TO YOU GOD OF HOSTS!” The worship is full throated, without restraint, nothing is held back. "PRAISE GOD ABOVE ALL CREATION."
When it’s finished it leaves behind its masterwork; every arc it threw remains and fills the space with a third dimension. A heavy blanket of smoke catches shafts of light, and the clouds still remain shifting on gentle air currents, enthroning God. The image the thurible leaves is striking; there's nothing in our mundane life that looks quite like it. The viewer is drawn out of the mundane world into the divine.

While the smoke covers and obfuscates, it also strangely focuses your gaze. You see less with your earthly eyes, so you must look deeper. The incense calls to see beyond the surface. Christ says "Cast out into deep water." Let your nets down deeper. There’s a strong surging current underneath the mundane. Let down your nets and feel this pull, strong and powerful. Your nets will feel near tearing with the power felt under the surface.

The thurible is passionate, artful. Some of the greatest Christian art ever produced is the temporary smoke of a thurible. It’s transporting. Its performance is lively and meaningful, deep and ancient. You see how God has been found inside incense since before Christianity. Ancient Judaism threw incense to find God, as did ancient pagan religions. Incense has always had the power to bring humans to a place where they seek and grasp for that eternal divinity.

Many modern churches have opted to replace the ancient thurible with the modern smoke machine. They've replaced the outdated device for something more modern. Thuribles are expensive, annoying to handle, and just don't feel "new". They think they can replace the pathos, the romance, the history with a plastic, unfeeling, mechanical smoke machine. The thurible is human, giving of itself via the swings of a priest. This clockwork could never replace the sublime worship of a thurible. Do they believe that C3H8O2 can replace incense? Remember the Tower of Babel? Humans in their vainglory try to reach heaven with man-made machinery. Incense was given and ordained by God himself for worship. Who are you lowly humans to use your glycerine golem?

The book of Daniel gives us verses praising God by reciting everything that gives him glory.

All you winds, bless the Lord,
Fire and heat, bless the Lord,
Cold and chill, bless the Lord,
Mountains and hills, bless the Lord,
Seas and rivers, bless the Lord,
All you water creatures, bless the Lord,
All you birds of the air, bless the Lord,
All you beasts, wild and tame, bless the Lord,
...

The natural world was created perfect by God. Nature, without the compromising force of humanity’s sinfulness, is our example of what perfect worship looks like. A deer scampers into the woods, it praises God. A fish nibbles algae from a rock, it praises God. Leaves fall from a tree. they praise God. There is no other way they could exist. They simply do. They repeat their routine, and in doing so God is honored.

Due to nature's perfection, churches are designed to emulate nature. Tall columns rise up like trees, the circular rose window resembles the sun. The building is so open you can see things far away, making you feel like you're more in a field than a city, and the openness isn't from a strictly utilitarian standpoint like a warehouse. The church is extravagant and maybe a little wasteful, to emulate nature. If you don't believe nature is extravagant and wasteful look outside at all the species of birds and plants. The world is 1000x more than it ever needed to be. And while we are looking at extravagance look at the walls of a church, all its ornate spirals and curves. The church's finer details are not the hard edges of civilization, but flowing and sometimes thorny like flowers. Try walking through a city, and on your walk enter a church. The church feels nothing like the city.

Of course churches aren't literally forests, they resemble nature. Churches are humanity using their intellect and creativity that was uniquely given to us by God. We share in God's ability to make good things. As God made the oceans, we made our society. As He set the stars, we made our laws. As he formed the animals we made our tools. And, of course, we made churches. And we made those churches to resemble nature. Of course we would, what else would we our art on. But we also have license to take our inspiration and run in a new direction. Our naturey churches don't have to literally have nature things in it. And you know what reader? You know what, to me, feels like it acts like nature? Smoke machines.
Going back a second, why is nature perfect? It's because nature isn't corrupted by free will. The way it was made simply glorifies God, in and of itself. The deer doesn't take center stage like a diva, neither do the fish, trees, wind, gravity, fire, frost, mountains, and hills. They do not take center stage, they just exist. You know what a smoke machine does?

What if the humble smoke machine, is actually more natural than the thurible. The thurible is artful and passionate, it would not relegate itself to second-fiddle. It will go and give its performance full throated. Nature is about the background, the fringes of your attention. Now you see where I've been going with all this. Did I trick you with that brutal takedown of smoke machines earlier? I’m kinda proud of that one. But really, if our churches are a forest, shouldn't they have a bird just chirping in the background giving glory to God, that's what a smoke machine does. It is the bird chirping in the background glorifying God. I like both the smoke machine and the thurible, but they feel different. One is passionate, the other is humble. It’s great that humanity can use its creativity to worship God in new ways. The smoke machine doesn’t have the history of the thurible, but it serves its own unique role, one the thurible never could, to exist unseen in the background, simply doing its role. Strangely, the smoke machine’s role feels more natural.

Certainly smoke machines are more prevalent among Protestant churches, not Catholic ones. That's Catholocism, it's a slow ship to turn. That's what makes the church so enduring, it thinks not in years but in centuries. Shoutout to that old adage "it takes 100 years to implement an ecumenical council." I think Catholics should more properly take this cue from our Protestant brothers and sisters. The smoke machine is nothing like the thurible, and that's ok. The smoke machine isn't some beautiful instrument thrown about as the focus of worship, it is, if anything, more like a choir. The choir doesn't sit behind the altar like some concert. The choir usually sits behind the faithful, contributing unseen. Smoke machines can be hidden behind plants and statues, tucked away. And at appropriate times, like at Adoration or any long period of worship, it can be deployed to enthrone God with smoke. After all, the church is ever ancient, ever new.
