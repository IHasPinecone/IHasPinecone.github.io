SPECIMEN #109

This dainty creature scuttles along the ground during the morning, feeding off smaller grubs and worms, and goes to sleep after the morning dew has evaporated. It sleeps in the nooks of trees until the night, when it takes to the sky. Seeing one of these creatures float, it looks like a light whisp on the air more than any fluttering insect. I havn't been able to study exactly how these creatures seem to float the way they do, it may be an illusion created by their wings, or possibly it's due to the unique shape of the wings themselves. While I can't describe how it is done, the look is enchanting. When they fly as wisps they are as delicate as a handkerchief floating along the breeze, yet still able to fully control their movement through the air, deftly dodging branches and leaves as if it were the most natural thing in the world.

And when two night moths encounter each other these whisps begin to sway back and forth, each adapting to the frequency of the other's swaying until they have fully synchronized their swaying. Simply two moths swaying together is remarkable, but what is truly exceptional is when a larger group of moths encounter each other in the night. They begin by swaying and synchronizing together, and the typical moth seems to settle into a simple background role, while other moths will break from the pattern to perform what I can only describe as performance. Most of the moths will form a background dance, simple swaying with a fixed period, but others will break from the pattern to twirl, flip, or begin swaying at a slightly odd, almost polyrhythmic pace, going from perfect synchronization to a special pattern that is beautiful to behold.

I do not know the reason these moths perform this ritual. At first I assumed it to be some sort of mating ritual, the performers are males trying to attract the background dancing females attention but as far as I've observed the moths do not perform any sexual contact after the dance is done, the group simply disperses after an amount of time. The amount of time the dance lasts has also been an object of curiosity. For the dances are getting longer, at a linear pace. I've managed to observe this dance on 29 occasions, and while time between when I successfully observed them is irregular when graphed on the date they observed the trend is increasingly linearly, down to the second.
(graph)

I've tried to tie this increase in time to anything I can think of, lunar cycles, the equinox, temperatures, none of this seems to fit. Shortly after arriving on this voyage 4 months ago one of my traveling companions went missing, a faint and delicate man of weak constitution, a student I agreed to let come along the journey as a favor to his father. We were traveling to a new location to make camp shortly after landing when someone in the night heard him stir in his tent. The man who heard him simply went back to bed, but when we arrived in the morning we saw his tent completely abandoned, he left all his supplies and clothes behind, as far as we know he only took the notebook he was keeping his notes in. You see, if one were to track the linear graph backward and find the time when the graph began at 0, it would be the night after he first disappeared.

I personally find this conclusion very concerning. If the moths have seemingly morphed their dances based on a time when my companion went missing that not only possibly invalidates much of the data I've collected if I cannot find the source, it also fills me with much worry. Could my companion have seen something that caused him to abandon our expedition to go into the jungle? When we searched the surrounding area we didn't find any signs he was attacked by a wild animal, or any signs of a struggle. It seems he saw something, and simply left, and the moths have increased their dancing relative to that.

SPECIMEN #031

I have been finding these dastardly bugs on my clothes since we've landed. They have an exceptional ability to cling to clothes and even worse; find folds of the clothes to hide in. More than once I have missed one before settling into my tent for the night. The bug then crawls out of my discarded coat and into the rest of my personal affects. The bug then leaves some horrible stench on every thing it crawls across in the night. I am usually awoken by the smell in the middle of the night and throw everything I can outside the tent in hope of evacuating the bug and stench from the tent. One time the bug had crawln all along the bottom of by cot, blasting my nostrils with the stench all night. The stench does dissipate very quickly after it has been applied, usually going to only a trance smell 12 hours after last contact with the insect.

The smell itself isn't from any massive secretion that I can determine from the bug itself, it is not as if the animal leaves behind an excrement that causes the stench, it's simply when the bug is in prolonged contact with the bug that causes it with no obvious explanation. We have also noted that the smell only seems to happen at night, creating the perfect storm where the bug cannot be detected during the day, and stinks up the tents at night.

I believe that this bug has warranted more study from members of the expedition simply to determine some way to clean the stench out of our clothes and gear. The men have gotten the bug to crawl on different materials, hoping to find some substance the bug cannot affect. Naturally, the bug does not leave the stench on any of the natural materials the men have tested: leaves, tree bark, earth, grass, vines, rocks, etc. This makes sense as the bug's stench is very rarely detected while out in the wilderness. The unfortunate fact is that the bug seems to affect almost every material we have brough with us on the expedition: fabrics, textiles, twine, canvas, steel, aluminum, glass, paper, leather, and every other material we have tried produces a reaction when in contact with the insect. It's almost as if the bug only reacts against synthetic materials.

Ultimately the bug is harmless. It doesn't bite or burrow or carry disease as far as we can tell, but it's been the largest nuisance to deal with since arriving. Before retiring to bed every night the men all carefully and methodically check every part of each others clothings looking for where one of these bugs has stayed. And if a bug is missed we all will learn about it the next morning because one of our compatriots will stink to high heaven and often drowsy as the horrible stench makes it incredibly difficult to sleep. There is nothing to be done about this besides just trying to find them before retiring for the night.

SPECIMEN #298

This specimen bears a striking horn and something unusual for species large horns, is usually seen in large groups. Traditionally, large horns are an indication of the male species, but in all our searching we have not found any of the insects without the horn. Indicating that females of this species are either difficult to find, or there is not the typical sexual dimorphism. One side effect of not finding any females is we’ve been unable to observe any mating habits of the insects since arriving.

One of the men observed while out cataloging native plants the prevalence of a specific unassuming green and brown fern found typically under canopies, and how the bugs are almost exclusively found near the fern, usually within only 50 yards. There is a strange theory among the expeditioners that the fern may actually be the female of the species. The fern itself has a rough inner stalk, with a tree-bark like appearance, often then males of the species will go and rub their horns against the stalk. We’ve never seen the fern react to this rubbing of the stalk, but the men have assumed this is how the female fern selects its mate, although we’ve never seen any sort of reaction.

There is one more notable feature of the fern worthy of documentation here that lends more credence to the reproductive theory: the flowering. We saw the remains of a dead fern, which bore the most beautiful flowers. It seems the fern flowered, and then fell dead of some unknown cause, and inside the flower were several dead tiny insects. While it’s hard to determine the species of larval insects, it would not surprise us if, had these larvae grown, they would be a member of this insect species. One of the men has been trying to determine the cause of death of this fern and was only able to come up with one reasonable excuse: this area was being observed last month by a group from our expedition, and one of the men had accidently spilled his coffee on the plant. This theoretically was harmful for the fern and so it tried to birth its children as quickly as possible and failed. The man who spilled the coffee has been reprimanded to not be so careless with his thermos in the future.

SPECIMEN #014

Upon first arriving this insect first endeared itself to many of us here. It’s droopy appearance is extremely endearing. However in the months we’ve been here this endearment has swiftly turned to loathing hatred. For this insect upon it’s arrival always marks the beginning of some sickness sweeping the men. Groups of this insect have only appeared 4 times, and each time a few days later at least half the men were bed ridden. Vomiting into buckets and woozy with some delusions and a fever. Given how rare the insect has appeared the men taken to treating its appearance as an omen. Some may call our correlation of these two events and disease unscientific due to a small sample size, but in our defense I will detail here one of the horrible nightmares one of the affected men shared with me.
